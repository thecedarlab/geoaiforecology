Research

Geospatial AI and remote sensing methods for forest disturbance mapping, landscape connectivity, spatial modeling, and biodiversity assessment.

My research sits at the intersection of geospatial artificial intelligence and ecology, developing computational methods to understand how forests and landscapes change and what those changes mean for the planet.

Research Areas

🛰️

Forest Disturbance Mapping

Automated detection of deforestation, degradation, and recovery using satellite time series and machine learning classifiers. Dense Landsat and Sentinel-2 stacks are used to pinpoint both the timing and the magnitude of change, so that gradual degradation is separated from abrupt clearing.

Change Detection Landsat Sentinel

🌍

Landscape Connectivity

Modeling how landscape structure and fragmentation affect species movement, gene flow, and ecosystem resilience. Graph-theoretic and circuit-based approaches turn land cover maps into networks whose weak links reveal conservation priorities.

Connectivity Graph Theory

📊

Spatial Modeling

Developing geospatial machine learning models for ecological prediction, including species distribution models and above-ground biomass estimation. Emphasis is on spatially aware validation, so that reported accuracy survives contact with new landscapes.

GeoAI Prediction

🌿

Biodiversity Assessment

Using multi-sensor remote sensing to characterize habitats and predict biodiversity patterns across spatial scales. Fusing optical, radar, and LiDAR data captures both the composition and the three-dimensional structure that species respond to.

Biodiversity LiDAR

Approach

  • Open by default
  • Freely available data
  • Built for practitioners

Code, data, and methods are released openly wherever licensing allows. Analyses are built as reproducible pipelines rather than one-off scripts, so results can be re-run, checked, and extended by others.

The methods here are designed around openly licensed Earth observation data — Landsat, Sentinel, SoilGrids, LANDFIRE, ESA WorldCover — so that they remain usable by researchers and practitioners without commercial imagery budgets.

Research outputs are packaged as tools people can actually run: QGIS plugins, R packages, and documented workflows, rather than results that stop at the figure in a paper.
